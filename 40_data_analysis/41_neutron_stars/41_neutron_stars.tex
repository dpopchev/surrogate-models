\subsection{Neutron Stars: Exploratory Data Analysis}
\label{sec:eda-ns}
\input{41_neutron_stars_numbers}

After filtering (Section~\ref{sec:neutron-stars}), the neutron-star table holds $\nsEdaRows$ configurations on $\nsEdaCurves$ curves, one per $(\beta, \lambda)$ pair. Each insight below states its answer first and then the evidence for it.

\paragraph{Central density enters as $\log_{10}\rhoc$, standardized.}
The surrogate takes $\log_{10}\rhoc$ as input and standardizes it to zero mean and unit variance (z-score), with the mean and standard deviation fitted on the training folds only. The density is positive and enters the structure equations multiplicatively, and the mass varies with $\log_{10}\rhoc$ as one smooth S-shaped curve per $(\beta, \lambda)$ pair (\cref{fig:ns-mass-density}). The sampled range is narrow, $\nsEdaRhocOrders$ orders of magnitude (\cref{tab:ns-univariate}). Every curve is sampled on one shared grid of $\nsEdaDensityGridValues$ densities, spaced about evenly in $\rhoc$ rather than in $\log_{10}\rhoc$. The marginal distribution is therefore not a bell: it is a plateau where all curves are present, which falls off as curves end at their maximum mass (\cref{fig:ns-univariate-continuous}). The skew changes from $\nsEdaRhocRawSkew$ to $\nsEdaRhocLogSkew$ under the logarithm (standard deviation $\nsEdaRhocLogStd$ in $\log_{10}$), but because of that shape the near-zero skew is weak evidence and is not used as the argument.

\paragraph{The charge target is $\log_{10}(\Dch/M)$.}
The surrogate regresses $\log_{10}(\Dch/M)$ instead of $\log_{10}\Dch$. The charge spans $\nsEdaDOrders$ orders of magnitude (from $\nsEdaDMin$ to $\nsEdaDMax$), so a logarithmic target is required; the question is what to divide by. Two correlations answer it (\cref{tab:ns-charge-correlation}). The Pearson coefficient measures how well one variable follows another along a straight line; the Spearman coefficient is the Pearson coefficient of the ranks, so it measures any monotonic dependence, linear or not. Dividing by the mass lowers the Spearman correlation with $M$ from $\nsEdaCorrMLogD$ to $\nsEdaCorrMLogDM$: almost all of the charge's monotonic dependence on the mass is removed, so the normalized charge carries information the mass does not, which suits one network predicting both. At the same time the Pearson correlation with $\lambda$ rises from $\nsEdaCorrLambdaLogD$ to $\nsEdaCorrLambdaLogDM$: the normalized charge follows a free parameter of the theory more linearly, which is easier to learn. \Cref{fig:ns-charge-target} shows the two targets against $M$.

\paragraph{Both free parameters are retained.}
$\beta$ and $\lambda$ are the free parameters of the theory, and both stay inputs. $\lambda$ carries the strongest linear signal in the charge (above); at fixed $\lambda$, $\beta$ still explains $\nsEdaBetaSharePercent\%$ of the variance of $\log_{10}(\Dch/M)$ (median over the $\lambda$ values).

\paragraph{The split groups whole curves: GroupKFold over $(\beta, \lambda)$, with the rim held out separately.}
Model selection uses GroupKFold over the $(\beta, \lambda)$ curves, which measures interpolation to unseen parameter pairs inside the sampled domain; extrapolation is measured separately on the rim curves and is not claimed as part of the model's validity. The rows of a curve are each other's nearest neighbours: for $\nsEdaSameCurvePercent\%$ of the rows the nearest other row, in standardized $(\beta, \lambda, \log_{10}\rhoc)$, lies on the same curve, at a median distance of $\nsEdaWithinMedian$ against $\nsEdaAcrossMedian$ to the nearest other curve (\cref{fig:ns-curve-adjacency}). A row-random split therefore leaks: a one-nearest-neighbour probe reaches a mean absolute error of $\nsEdaSplitRandomRowsDM$ on $\log_{10}(\Dch/M)$ when rows are held out at random, against $\nsEdaSplitCurvesDM$ when whole curves are (\cref{tab:ns-split-strategies}, $\nsEdaFolds$ folds). Holding out whole $\beta$ lines costs the same as holding out curves ($\nsEdaSplitBetaLinesDM$), because the nearest neighbour of a curve is the adjacent $\beta$ at the same $\lambda$; holding out whole $\lambda$ lines is much harder ($\nsEdaSplitLambdaLinesDM$), and predicting the rim from the interior is hardest ($\nsEdaSplitRimDM$). For the mass the ordering reverses ($\nsEdaSplitRandomRowsM$ random against $\nsEdaSplitCurvesM$ grouped): all curves share the density grid and the mass depends mostly on $\rhoc$, so the neighbouring curve at the same density predicts it better than the next density on the same curve. Grouping by curve is the honest test of what the surrogate is for, prediction at parameter pairs it has not seen.

\paragraph{The domain is triangular and unevenly sampled.}
The model is valid on the filled $(\beta, \lambda)$ region and the sampled density range. Only $\nsEdaGridFillPercent\%$ of the $\nsEdaGridCells$ cells of the parameter grid hold a converged curve ($\nsEdaCurves$): the corner of large $\beta$ and large $\lambda$ is empty, and the number of rows per curve falls from $\nsEdaRowsPerCurveMax$ at small $\lambda$ to $\nsEdaRowsPerCurveMin$ near the boundary (\cref{fig:ns-grid-fill}).

\paragraph{The data pile up near the maximum mass.}
The largest mass in the dataset is $\nsEdaMassMax\,\Msun$, and $\nsEdaPileUpPercent\%$ of the rows lie within $\nsEdaPileUpWindow\,\Msun$ of their own curve's maximum (\cref{fig:ns-mass-max}). The densities are spaced about evenly while $dM/d\rhoc \to 0$ at the turning point, so many grid points map onto nearly the same mass; this concentration is a sampling artefact, and it makes the region around $\Mmax$ both heavily weighted and the hardest to resolve (Hypothesis~$H_2$).
