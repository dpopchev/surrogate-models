\subsection{Black Holes: Exploratory Data Analysis}
\label{sec:eda-bh}

The black-hole table holds $\bhEdaRows$ configurations on $\bhEdaCurves$ curves, one per $\beta$, each sampled densely in the horizon radius $\rh$. Its answers differ from the neutron stars' in three of five places (1, 3 and 4), and its data allow far more precision (6):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the horizon radius enters linearly, standardized;
  \item the mass target is $M$ itself, in solar masses;
  \item dividing the charge by the mass brings no improvement, unlike for neutron stars;
  \item $\beta$ barely changes $M$ or $\Dch$ at fixed $\rh$; it sets where each curve starts;
  \item the split groups whole $\beta$ curves: GroupKFold over $\beta$;
  \item the spacing between curves bounds the reachable error, here far less than for neutron stars: about $\bhEdaCeilMFigures$ significant figures for the mass and $\bhEdaCeilDFigures$ for the charge.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{1.\ The horizon radius enters linearly, standardized.}
$\rh$ spans only $\bhEdaRhOrders$ orders of magnitude, from $\bhEdaRhMin$ to $\bhEdaRhMax$, on a uniform grid of step $\bhEdaRhStep$, and its distribution is flat with a skew of $\bhEdaRhRawSkew$ (\cref{fig:bh-univariate-continuous}, \cref{tab:bh-univariate}). A logarithm would only make it skewed, so $\rh$ enters as it is and is standardized on the training folds.

\paragraph{2.\ The mass target is $M$, in solar masses.}
The mass ranges from $\bhEdaMMin$ to $\bhEdaMMax\,\Msun$ and follows the horizon radius almost exactly, with Pearson $r = \bhEdaPearsonMRh$, one nearly straight line per $\beta$ curve (\cref{fig:bh-mass-radius}). It is bounded and of order one, so it enters as it is, like the neutron-star mass. The dilaton shows only near the existence edge: there the curves leave the general-relativistic relation $M = \rh/2$ and fan out by $\beta$ (\cref{fig:bh-mass-radius}, inset), while for larger $\rh$ they merge into one line.

\paragraph{3.\ Dividing the charge by the mass brings no improvement.}
For neutron stars, $\log_{10}(\Dch/M)$ separated the charge from the mass (Section~\ref{sec:eda-ns}); for black holes it does not. The charge is already almost a function of the mass alone: the Spearman correlation with $M$ is $\bhEdaSpearmanMLogD$ for $\log_{10}\Dch$ and stays at $\bhEdaSpearmanMLogDM$ for $\log_{10}(\Dch/M)$ (\cref{tab:bh-charge-correlation}, \cref{fig:bh-charge-target}). The range is also narrow, $\bhEdaDOrders$ orders of magnitude for $\Dch$ and $\bhEdaDMOrders$ for $\Dch/M$, so a logarithm serves to reduce skew rather than to span a range; the target is fixed with the decisions in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\paragraph{4.\ $\beta$ sets where each curve starts, not what it holds.}
At fixed $\rh$, changing $\beta$ moves the mass by only $\bhEdaBetaEffectPercentM\%$ of its spread and $\log_{10}\Dch$ by $\bhEdaBetaEffectPercentLD\%$. Its role is the existence edge: the smallest horizon radius rises from $\bhEdaEdgeRhMinLow$ at $\beta = \bhEdaEdgeBetaLow$ to $\bhEdaEdgeRhMinHigh$ at $\beta = \bhEdaEdgeBetaHigh$ (\cref{fig:bh-existence-edge}). $\beta$ therefore stays an input, and the domain of validity is the region above the edge.
\review{whether the black-hole and neutron-star datasets come from the same theory; $\beta$ spans $\bhEdaEdgeBetaLow$ to $\bhEdaEdgeBetaHigh$ here and $0.4$ to $49.4$ for neutron stars, and matters far less here.}

\paragraph{5.\ The split groups whole $\beta$ curves.}
Each curve is so dense that every row's nearest neighbour lies on its own curve ($\bhEdaSameCurvePercent\%$; median distance $\bhEdaWithinMedian$ against $\bhEdaAcrossMedian$ to the next curve), so a random split of rows leaks. A one-nearest-neighbour probe (\cref{tab:bh-split-strategies}, $\bhEdaFolds$ folds) shows it for the charge, which $\beta$ affects most: $\log_{10}\Dch$ scores $\bhEdaSplitRandomRowsLD$ with rows held out at random against $\bhEdaSplitCurvesLD$ with whole curves. Holding out the outermost curves scores as holding out any curve ($\bhEdaSplitOuterCurvesLD$), so extrapolating in $\beta$ is no harder than interpolating; as for neutron stars, the mass alone reverses the order ($\bhEdaSplitRandomRowsM$ against $\bhEdaSplitCurvesM$), because all curves share one $\rh$ grid.

\paragraph{6.\ The data bound the reachable error.}
The same test as for neutron stars -- each interior curve predicted from the two curves on each side, a cubic spline in $\beta$ after interpolating each neighbour along $\rh$ -- gives the error a smooth interpolation of the $\beta$ grid already reaches (\cref{tab:bh-ceilings}, \cref{fig:bh-ceilings}). The noise along a curve is $\bhEdaCeilMNoise$ for the mass and $\bhEdaCeilDNoise$ for the charge (median), a spline through every other row predicts the rows between to $\bhEdaCeilMAlong$ and $\bhEdaCeilDAlong$, and across curves the 95th percentile of the error is $\bhEdaCeilMAcrossBeta$ for the mass and $\bhEdaCeilDAcrossBeta$ for the charge: about $\bhEdaCeilMFigures$ and $\bhEdaCeilDFigures$ significant figures. As an uncertainty of the predicted values, at the median mass $M = \bhEdaCeilMAt\,\Msun$ and the median charge $\Dch = \bhEdaCeilDAt$ this bound is $\bhEdaCeilMSigma\,\Msun$ and $\bhEdaCeilDSigma$, or $\bhEdaCeilDSigmaDex$ in $\log_{10}\Dch$, at the 95th percentile: a floor on a surrogate's error, to be set beside the observational uncertainty in the inference of Section~\ref{sec:mcmc}. The error is not spread evenly: it rises by orders of magnitude at the smallest radii, where each curve starts at its own existence edge (decision~4), and stays near the noise everywhere else. Even with only $\bhEdaCurves$ curves, interpolation between them reaches this bound; whether a surrogate keeps it between curves it was not trained on, and near the existence edge, is the black-hole question for Section~\ref{sec:results}.
